# Introduction
The human gut microbiome is a complex ecosystem of trillions of microorganisms that play a critical role in host metabolism, immune modulation, and disease prevention (De Filippis et al., 2019). Among the most influential determinants of gut microbiome composition is diet. The macronutrient and fibre content of habitual dietary patterns shapes the availability of substrates for microbial fermentation, which in turn selects for specific microbial taxa and metabolic pathways (Losasso et al., 2018). The study conducted by David et al. (2014) showed that dietary interventions can rapidly alter the microbial landscape. Vegans, who consume exclusively plant-based foods, typically harbor a microbiome enriched in fiber-degrading bacteria whereas omnivorous diets, by contrast, include greater proportions of animal-derived proteins and saturated fats, which have been shown to favour proteolytic bacteria and alter bile acid metabolism in the distal gut (Sonnenburg & Bäckhed, 2016). Disruptions to microbiome composition (between host and gut microbiome) which is referred to as dysbiosis have been linked and associated with various medical conditions including cancer, obesity, neurological disorders as well as malnutrition (Gonzalez et al., 2011; Sonnenburg & Bäckhed, 2016; Turnbaugh et al., 2008).  
Shotgun metagenomics is a powerful approach for characterising gut microbial communities, offering several advantages over amplicon-based methods such as 16S rRNA gene sequencing. While 16S sequencing targets a single hypervariable region of the ribosomal RNA gene and is limited in taxonomic resolution at the species level, shotgun metagenomics sequences all DNA present in a sample, enabling strain-level taxonomic classification, functional gene annotation, and the detection of novel organisms not represented in 16S reference databases (Quince et al., 2017). These properties make shotgun metagenomics the preferred method for studies seeking high-resolution taxonomic and functional profiling of complex microbial communities.
The specific aim of this analysis is to compare the gut microbiome composition of vegans to omnivores, while focusing on differences in taxonomic abundance, alpha diversity, beta diversity and also differential species abundance. This work contributes to a growing body of evidence linking dietary patterns to microbiome composition and its potential consequences for human health.
# Methodology
## Data Source and Processing
Raw data were obtained from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession SRP126540. Six samples were selected for analysis: three as Omnivore (SRR8146935, SRR8146936, SRR8146938) and three as vegan (SRR8146937, SRR8146943, SRR8146944). All samples were paired-end Illumina sequencing reads with a read length of 150 base pairs. Sample metadata confirming dietary group was also created.
## Quality Control
Raw sequencing data were downloaded from NCBI SRA using fasterq-dump v3.0.9 (SRA Toolkit; NCBI) on the Digital Research Alliance of Canada (DRAC) Narval high-performance computing cluster. The --split-files flag was used to separate paired-end reads into forward (_1) and reverse (_2) FASTQ files, and all output files were immediately compressed using gzip to reduce storage requirements. Quality control of raw reads was performed using FastQC v0.12.1 (Andersson et al., 2010) and results were aggregated across all samples using MultiQC. Various metrics were reviewed to confirm if the data is good and probably need to be trimmed using a fastp. All samples demonstrated zero adapter contamination, and no trimming was deemed necessary prior to classification.
## Taxonomic Classification
Taxonomic classification was performed using Kraken2 v2.1.6 (Wood et al., 2019) against the standard Kraken2 database (k2_standard_20260226), which includes complete genomes from bacteria, archaea, viruses, and the human reference genome. Species-level abundance estimation was subsequently performed using Bracken v3.0 (Lu et al., 2017) with a read length parameter of 150 bp. The Bracken species-level reports from all six samples were converted to BIOM format using kraken-biom v1.2 (Ng & Gutaker, 2025), producing a single merged abundance table for import into R.
## Diversity and Differential Abundance Analysis
The BIOM file was imported into R through phyloseq alongside sample metadata and every other downstream analysis followed (McMurdie & Holmes, 2013). For Alpha diversity, Observed species richness, Chao1, Shannon, invSimpson and Simpson's index were calculated on rarefied count tables using phyloseq, with rarefaction performed to the minimum sample depth to ensure comparable sequencing effort across samples. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity calculated on rarefied counts, and community structure was visualized by Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) using phyloseq. Statistical significance of group-level compositional differences was tested using PERMANOVA (Kelly et al., 2015). Differential abundance analysis between vegan and omnivore samples was performed using DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014) on unrarefied counts with positive-count size factor estimation. Taxa with adjusted p-value below 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold-change greater than 1 were considered statistically significant. All figures were generated using ggplot2.

## Alpha and Beta Diversity
Alpha diversity analysis on rarefied count data revealed consistently higher diversity indices in vegan samples compared to omnivore samples across all five metrics (Figure 9). Omnivore samples demonstrated higher mean Observed species, Chao1, Shannon, Fischer and Simpson's index. The limited sample size (n=3 per group) reduces statistical power and likely contributes to non-significant results for several metrics. Beta diversity analysis using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity revealed no statistically significant difference in gut microbial community composition between vegan and omnivore participants. PCoA ordination showed partial separation between groups along the primary axis of variation (Axis 1 = 60.1%, Axis 2 = 18.1%), suggesting a trend toward compositional divergence associated with dietary pattern. Jaccard PCoA (Figure 11), which considers only taxon presence and absence, showed minimal separation between groups (Axis 1 = 47%, Axis 2 = 19.9%). Bray-Curtis NMDS ordination (Figure 12) corroborated these patterns, though one omnivore sample appeared as a compositional outlier, likely reflecting individual-level dietary or lifestyle variation within the omnivore group. However, table 1; PERMANOVA analysis indicated that diet group explained only 10.7% of the variance in Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (R² = 0.107, F = 0.479, p = 0.70, complete enumeration of 719 permutations), which was not statistically significant. It should be noted that with only three samples per group, the total number of unique permutations is limited to 719, substantially constraining statistical power and the minimum achievable p-value.

## Differential Abundance analysis
DESeq2 differential abundance analysis identified a number of taxa that were significantly significantly enriched in vegan participants relative to omnivores (padj < 0.05). FSL E2-0274 (log2FC = 24.85, padj = 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁶).

# Discussion
This study employed shotgun metagenomics to characterise and compare the gut microbiome composition of vegan and omnivore individuals, revealing significant differences in taxonomic abundance, beta diversity, and differential species abundance. These findings contribute to a growing body of literature demonstrating that long-term dietary patterns are among the most influential determinants of gut microbial community structure (Fackelmann et al., 2025; Sonnenburg & Bäckhed, 2016). The most prominent taxon enriched in vegan participants was Prevotella copri, a species belonging to the phylum Bacteroidota that is strongly associated with high-fibre, plant-based dietary patterns. Prevotella copri is a specialist degrader of complex plant polysaccharides including hemicellulose and pectin, producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as propionate as fermentation end-products(De Filippis et al., 2019). SCFAs are the principal energy source for colonocytes and exert broad anti-inflammatory effects on the intestinal mucosa through inhibition of histone deacetylases and activation of G-protein coupled receptors (Koh et al., 2016) The enrichment of Prevotella copri in vegan and omnivore participants is therefore consistent with greater availability of fermentable fibre substrates and suggests a gut environment characterised by higher SCFA production capacity (De Filippis et al., 2019). However, it is worth noting that some studies have associated Prevotella copri with inflammatory conditions including rheumatoid arthritis, highlighting the context-dependent nature of its effects (Maier et al., 2018).
As noted by Willis (2019), rarefaction is a prerequisite for comparing diversity. Without reaching a plateau, any difference in "Richness" between Vegans and Omnivores could just be an artifact of one sample having more reads than another. It can be seen that the samples plateau at different heights. Some researchers rarefy their data to the same depth before calculating diversity. However, McMurdie and Holmes (2014) argue that rarefying can be statistically inefficient. 
The results show a high abundance of Bacillota in both groups, and slightly similar in Bacteroidota proportion in Vegans. In research conducted by Ley et al. (2005) and updated by Tomova et al. (2019), the ratio of these two phyla is highly sensitive to dietary intake. A plant-based diet typically increases the richness of Bacteroidota because many members of this phylum possess a vast array of Polysaccharide Utilization Loci (PULs) dedicated to breaking down complex plant glycans. Another phyla that is worthy of note is the little slice on the vegan and omnivores is the Actinomycetota which houses the genus Bifidobacterium, a beneficial bacteria thrives on the prebiotic fibers (inulin, oligofructose) found in onions, garlic, and whole grains of a vegan diet (Franco-de-Moraes et al., 2017).
A quick dive into the family which provides much deeper biological insight into how diet shapes the gut. Vegan group shows an expansion of Prevotellaceae. De Filippo et al. (2010) famously compared European children to rural African children, finding that high-fiber diets select for Prevotellaceae while "Western" diets select for Bacteroidaceae. 
For the genera, the veganand omnivore bars seem to host the segatella genus which was recently reclassified from prevotella family.  The higher level seen in Alistipes for the omnivore is a direct biological response to fat intake. Caricilli and Saad (2014)found that Alistipes (such as Alistipes putredinis) blooms almost immediately when subjects switch to an animal-based diet because it thrives on the bile acids required to emulsify animal fats.
Species-level data seem to be the highest resolution as it displayed various numbers of species each appearing significantly with copri taking the highest in both diet. In the Omnivore bars, species like Bacteroides vulgatus, Wu et al. (2011) noted that high levels of Bacteroides species are consistently linked to long-term intake of animal proteins and saturated fats, as they are highly tolerant of the bile acids secreted to digest these foods.
Diversity analysis measures the richness and evenness within each group. While the previous bar plots showed the taxa, this plot quantifies the complexity of those communities. Some studies, such as Tomova et al. (2019) report slightly higher alpha diversity in vegans due to the variety of plant-based glycans. However, David et al. (2014) demonstrated that the type of diversity changes much faster than the amount of diversity. The results support the idea that an omnivorous diet is not necessarily "less diverse," but rather "differently diverse. With only three biological replicates per group, the statistical power to detect a significant difference in alpha diversity is low. As discussed by Kelly et al. (2015), high inter-individual variation in human subjects often masks dietary signals in alpha diversity metrics unless the sample size is significantly larger. For the beta diversity, PCoA plot is the definitive measure, showing how different the entire microbial communities are from one another that it visualizes the distance between the Vegan and Omnivore group. In contrast to this study, the American Gut Project found significant beta diversity shifts (p < 0.05) when comparing plant-rich diets to animal-rich diets across thousands of people (McDonald et al., 2018). This highlights that this result is likely limited by statistical power. Jaccard PCoA minimal separation between groups indicated that vegan and omnivore participants share a broadly similar core gut microbiome in terms of taxon membership, with differences primarily manifesting at the level of relative abundance. Bray-Curtis NMDS ordination corroborated these patterns, though one omnivore sample appeared as a compositional outlier, likely reflecting individual-level dietary or lifestyle variation within the omnivore group. The non-significant PERMANOVA result (R² = 0.107, p = 0.70) is a statistical limitation imposed by the small sample size of this study (n = 3 per group). This is a well-recognised limitation of permutation-based multivariate tests in microbiome studies with small sample sizes (Kelly et al., 2015). Kelly et al. (2015) demonstrated through simulation studies that microbiome PERMANOVA analyses require a minimum of 10–20 samples per group to achieve adequate statistical power for detecting moderate effect sizes.
The differential abundance analysis revealed striking asymmetry in the statistical significance of omnivore-enriched versus vegan-enriched taxa, with omnivore-associated organisms displaying higher significance scores despite similar fold change magnitudes on both sides of the volcano plot. The presence of taxa with extreme fold changes on both sides of the volcano plot corroborates findings from David et al. (2014), who demonstrated using controlled dietary interventions that animal-based and plant-based diets rapidly and reproducibly select for distinct microbial populations, with certain taxa essentially confined to one dietary environment. Furthermore, Wastyk et al. (2021) reported in a randomised dietary intervention that high-fibre plant-based diets consistently enriched fibre-degrading Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae families represented among the vegan-enriched taxa in the present study while animal protein consumption promoted proteolytic Bacteroidaceae and bile-tolerant Proteobacteria, consistent with the omnivore-enriched taxa observed here. The large number of significantly differentially abundant taxa identified despite the small sample size underscores the magnitude of diet-driven compositional differences between these two dietary groups and highlights the potential of shotgun metagenomics to resolve diet-microbiome associations at species-level resolution (Quince et al., 2017)

# Conclusion
In conclusion, this shotgun metagenomics analysis demonstrates that vegan and omnivore individuals harbour compositionally distinct gut microbial communities, characterised by differential representation of ecologically and clinically relevant taxa. Vegan participants showed enrichment of plant-fibre-degrading, butyrate-producing organisms associated with anti-inflammatory and mucoprotective functions, while omnivore participants showed greater representation of taxa linked to animal-fat and protein metabolism.
